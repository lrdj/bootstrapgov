# What research found (and would likely find again)

LIP ran 25+ visits plus structured research rounds with farmers, marts, abattoirs, hauliers, tag manufacturers, APHA and local-authority officers, keepers with support needs and third-party software users. Nine themes recurred.

## 1. Paper persists because the system makes it rational

Reporting a move electronically still required printing forms; hauliers and abattoirs kept paper as a failsafe; a high-tech abattoir still printed copies of everything "just in case". Digitising a step without removing the paper obligation delivers the worst of both.

## 2. The physical layer undermines the digital one

Ear tags fail, fall out, or aren't fitted until animals leave the farm; pig slap-marks smudge; markets are permitted to miss a percentage when scanning. Enforcement officers called this out directly: traceability fails when markets are allowed to miss-scan animals.

## 3. Enforcement officers didn't trust the movement data

One officer's spot-check of three markets found totals hundreds adrift; extensive double bookkeeping; "ringing" (identity-swapping) in sheep; incomplete moves caused by postcode-vs-CPH mismatches. They used ARAMS as an enforcement tool, not a source of truth.

## 4. Complexity breeds over-compliance and error

Keepers couldn't tell which records were essential, so they recorded everything; one hobby keeper with ~50 sheep maintained six flock registers. Too many identifiers; nobody knows what they're all for.

## 5. Whole-move, one-party reporting matters

The on/off half-movement model (a strict 1998 interpretation of EU law) generates anomalies other member states avoid by letting one keeper report the whole move.

## 6. What people liked is instructive

The pig text-notification service, the BCMS helpline, and CTS online generally.

## 7. Digital exclusion is real but nuanced

Rural broadband gaps, keepers with support needs (a dedicated research round), and the "wellies test" — if data entry requires going into the farmhouse and washing your hands, it will not happen at the point of movement.

## 8. Cross-border moves were already a pain point in 2017

Keepers wanted seamless England–Scotland moves; lifetime traceability across UK internal borders needed data integration that didn't exist.

## 9. Sheep EID (2012) is the cautionary tale for technology without service change

The promised benefit — carcase feedback from abattoirs to producers — never materialised because industry practice didn't change. NI's beef sector showed the prize: 5–7% productivity gains just from sharing end-product data back to breeders.

## The personas hold up as hypotheses

The team's personas remain good hypotheses to retest: the sheep farmer drowning in ARAMS errors, the multi-species keeper juggling passports, registers and census returns, the mart, the high-tech abattoir, and the local-authority enforcement officer.
